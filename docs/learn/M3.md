## Concepts

- **The EVM is a stack machine.** Opcodes pop and push words. A raw `ADD` wraps around where Solidity 0.8 reverts, and an invalid opcode or an empty stack burns all the gas while `REVERT` keeps what is left.
- **A call is found by its first four bytes.** The selector is the start of the hash of the signature, and the rest of the calldata is one word per argument. A selector that matches nothing runs the fallback or reverts.
- **A revert undoes the call that reverted, and the access list with it.** A caller that catches it keeps its own earlier writes, and a slot touched inside the reverted call is cold again.
- **Call, delegatecall and staticcall differ in whose storage and sender they use.** Delegatecall runs the code against the caller's storage by slot, not by name, which is what the later account modules rely on.
- **State has a price list.** A first write to an empty slot costs 22100 with the cold access, a change to a filled one 5000, and a read 2100 or 100. A refund is capped at a fifth of the gas used, memory grows quadratically, a log costs 375 plus 375 a topic plus 8 a byte, and transient storage costs 100.
- **Calldata is priced twice.** 4 gas a zero byte and 16 a non-zero one, unless the call does so little that the EIP-7623 floor of 10 a token is higher.
- **ERC-20 is a ledger inside a contract.** The standard leaves open the zero address, an infinite allowance and the order of checks, and each choice is a trap or a saving.
- **Properties are checked three ways.** A unit test checks one scenario, a fuzz test checks one function with random input, and an invariant test checks a property after any sequence of calls, with a handler and a model so the sequences mean something.

## What the tests taught

- **S1:** a call from the test contract costs about 21,200 gas even when the code is a single `STOP`. This stayed unexplained until S6.
- **S2:** the optimizer deletes a read whose result is unused and merges two writes to one slot, so a gas meter built on them measured 2 gas instead of 2100. The values have to be used. A refund came back as 8,880 instead of 19,900 because EIP-3529's cap of a fifth of the gas used applied, and `forge coverage` builds without the optimizer, so every bound has to hold in both builds.
- **S3:** the approve race is real: a spender who acts between an allowance of 50 and one of 30 spends 80.
- **S4:** OpenZeppelin refuses the zero address, never spends a maximum allowance and reports ERC-6093 errors. It also keeps the approve race.
- **S5:** the invariant that the zero address holds nothing was vacuous until four actions targeted it. Foundry replays a persisted failing sequence first, which made a check I had written fail on every short replay and made the first round of mutation results meaningless. The check became a deterministic test and the cache is cleared between mutants.
- **S6:** Foundry charges a call that the test contract makes like a transaction: the 21,000 base and the calldata, with no cold-access charge for the contract it calls. That explains S1's figure, and calls between contracts are not charged. A near-empty call with 100 non-zero bytes costs 3,000 more than with 100 zero bytes, which is the EIP-7623 floor at 30 a byte, while a call that does real work pays the 12 a byte of the standard price. A transfer to a fresh address costs exactly 17,100 more than to a holder, and OpenZeppelin costs within about 33 gas of the bare token on a transfer. Its skipped allowance write saves about 3,200 gas on a spend with a maximum allowance.

## Check questions

1. **How a call runs.** Why does `ADD` on the maximum wrap around in raw bytecode but revert in Solidity 0.8? What do `INVALID` and `REVERT` each do to the gas left? If a caller catches a reverting inner call, which of its writes survive, and what does the revert do to the gas price of a slot the inner call touched?
2. **What state costs.** Why does a first write to an empty slot cost 22,100 while a change to a filled one costs 5,000 and a read costs 100? Why does a transfer to a fresh address cost 17,100 more than one to a holder? What is the refund for clearing a slot, and why was a refund of 19,900 reported as 8,880?
3. **ERC-20 traps.** What does EIP-20 leave open that the bare token got wrong in a way that matters, and how does OpenZeppelin fix it? How does the approve race work, and what should a client do about it? What is the trade-off of an infinite allowance, in gas and in risk?
4. **Testing a property.** What is the difference between a unit, a fuzz and an invariant test? Why does an invariant test need a handler and a reference model, and why is `fail_on_revert` on? What went wrong with the first round of mutation results, and what does that say about trusting a passing test?
5. **Gas from a test.** Why does a call from the test contract cost about 21,000 gas more than the same call from another contract? When does the calldata floor of EIP-7623 decide the price and when does the standard price? Why must a gas bound hold in the unoptimized build too?

## Answers

Written by Claude at the user's request. Read them as a reference, not as a record of what the user understood.

1. **How a call runs.** `ADD` is defined on 256-bit words modulo 2^256, so the maximum plus one is zero with no flag: the opcode has no failure mode. Solidity 0.8 adds a check after the addition, that the result is not smaller than an operand, and reverts with `Panic(0x11)` for a few opcodes more; `unchecked` removes it. `INVALID` and the other exceptional halts, an empty stack, a bad jump or running out of gas, end the frame and burn all the gas it was given. `REVERT` ends it, undoes its changes, returns the revert data and keeps the unused gas: in the lab `INVALID` burned the whole 100,000 while `REVERT` cost 6 gas more than `STOP`. When a caller catches a reverting inner call, the inner call's writes are undone, its logs and transient writes with them, while the caller's own earlier writes survive: `writeThenCatch` ends with the value 2 and `caught` true. The slot the inner call touched goes cold again, because EIP-2929 rolls the access list back with the scope, so reading it costs 2,100 and not 100.
2. **What state costs.** The prices follow the work and what stays: a first write to an empty slot adds permanent state that every node stores, 20,000; changing a filled slot is 2,900; and the first touch of a slot in a transaction costs 2,100 more for a cold read, after which it is 100. So 22,100 is 20,000 plus the cold access, 5,000 is 2,900 plus the cold access, and a read of a warm slot is 100. A transfer to a fresh address sets the recipient's balance slot where a transfer to a holder only changes it, which is 20,000 against 2,900, so 17,100 more, and the test asserts exactly that for both tokens. Clearing a slot refunds 4,800 by EIP-3529, and a refund is capped at a fifth of the gas used. The small call used 44,404 gas, a fifth of which is 8,880, below the 19,900 the restore rule gives, so 8,880 was reported. With six padding writes the gas used grows, the cap stops binding and 19,900 appears.
3. **ERC-20 traps.** EIP-20 does not forbid the zero address, so the bare token loses what is sent to it and the supply stays the same; OpenZeppelin reverts with `ERC20InvalidReceiver` and `ERC20InvalidSpender`. The approve race: Alice changes an allowance from 50 to 30, the spender sees the change waiting and spends 50 first, Alice's approval then lands and the spender spends 30 more, 80 in all. OpenZeppelin v5 keeps it. A client should set the allowance to zero first and wait for that to confirm, which EIP-20 advises and a contract cannot enforce without breaking older tokens, or approve only what one use needs. An infinite allowance saves about 3,200 gas per spend, since OpenZeppelin skips the allowance write (18,557 against 15,320), and spares re-approving; the risk is that a spender that is compromised or buggy can take the whole balance, for ever. That is why the agent wallet in M4 and M5 will enforce limits in the account and not rely on the allowance.
4. **Testing a property.** A unit test checks one scenario written by hand, a fuzz test checks one function with random inputs against a property, and an invariant test checks properties after random sequences of calls. Raw random calls mostly revert or reach meaningless states, so a handler bounds the inputs, fixes the actors, favours amounts at the boundaries and includes the zero address, and a reference model written from the rules gives an independent expected result, including the exact revert data, so the token is checked against the specification and not against itself. `fail_on_revert` is on because otherwise a revert in the handler is silently discarded and the property goes unchecked on that sequence. In the first round of mutation results, Foundry replayed a persisted failing sequence first and a check I had written failed on every short replay, so every mutant after the first looked killed. A test result says nothing until you know why it came out that way: the kill has to be for the right reason, so clear the caches, confirm the mutant compiles, and run only the tests being judged.
5. **Gas from a test.** Foundry charges a call the test contract makes as a transaction, 21,000 plus the calldata with no cold-access charge for the callee, because such a call stands for a transaction, while a call between contracts costs only its execution: a `STOP` called from another contract cost 2,715 and from the test 21,350. By EIP-7623 a transaction pays 21,000 plus the larger of four gas a token plus execution and ten gas a token, where a zero byte is one token and a non-zero byte four. The floor decides when execution is small beside the calldata: a near-empty contract called with 1,000 zero bytes pays 31,000 by the floor against 25,000 by the standard price, and 100 non-zero bytes cost 3,000 more than 100 zero bytes, 30 a byte. A call that does real work, such as an `approve`, pays the standard price, where a non-zero byte costs 12 more and 31 of them cost 372 more. A gas bound has to hold in the unoptimized build because `forge coverage`, which CI runs, compiles without the optimizer and the same code uses more gas: the Solidity `emit` cost 206 gas over the bare log there against a bound of 150. A bound tuned to the optimized build would fail the job, so the tests assert relations and ranges that hold in both.
